A neural network reads 10-second EEG windows and outputs the probability of a seizure. A neurologist wants to know why it flagged one window. LIME explains a single prediction: it perturbs the input many times and fits a simple linear model to how the black box responds. For EEG, what is the instance, what are its parts, and what does it mean to remove one?

Stage 1 — The Instance and Its Parts

The instance is the window the model classifies: 3 channels × 10 seconds. Split each channel into four 2.5-second segments; each channel × segment is one interpretable component. Click a component to remove it (it is replaced by that channel's normal background EEG) and watch the model's output.

Stage 2 — Sample, Weigh, Fit

LIME draws random neighbours: copies of the window with a random set of components removed. It runs the model on each one and weights it by its closeness, π = exp(−d²/σ²), where d is the share of components removed and σ = 0.25. Then it fits a weighted linear model to the outputs. The coefficients are the explanation.

Takeaway
  • Instance and components: the instance is the window the model classifies (here 3 channels × 10 s). Components are channel × time segments, units a neurologist can read: "left temporal, 2.5–5 s".
  • LIME removes random sets of components, runs the model on each neighbour, weights neighbours by their closeness to x, and fits a linear model. Its coefficients explain the prediction locally (Ribeiro et al., 2016).
  • It is an estimate: with too few neighbours the ranking changes from draw to draw. Check that it is stable before you trust it.
  • Removing must look realistic. Flat zeros look like electrode faults, are out of distribution, and spread the credit everywhere. Background EEG keeps the neighbours plausible.
  • Agreement with a neurologist supports the model but does not prove it. Check many windows, including the model's errors.
