"dog bites man" and "man bites dog" use the same words with opposite meanings. A transformer's self-attention compares every word with every other word through dot products, and nothing in it refers to a word's position. So where does word order come in?

Q1 What does attention do when the words are shuffled?
Q2 How do sinusoidal encodings mark positions, and what do their dot products show?
Q3 How do learned, relative, ALiBi and rotary encodings differ?

Why these questions: they explain why transformers need positional information and how the main methods differ.

Stage 3 — ALiBi: A Penalty on Distance

ALiBi adds no position vectors at all. It subtracts m·|i − j| from each attention score, where m is a fixed slope that differs between heads. Here a word attends to itself and to the 5 words before it, all with equal content scores, so only the penalty tells them apart.

Takeaway
  • Self-attention ignores order: shuffling the words shuffles the outputs in the same way (permutation equivariance). Without position information, "dog" gets the same output in "dog bites man" and "man bites dog".
  • Sinusoidal PE adds sin/cos pairs that turn at different speeds. Every PE(p) has the same length, and PE(p)·PE(p + k) depends only on the offset k, so a pattern like "look 2 back" looks the same at every position.
  • Absolute methods (sinusoidal, learned) add a vector to the input, and a learned table has no row beyond its training length. Relative methods (T5 bias, ALiBi, RoPE) put i − j into the attention score.
  • ALiBi's fixed penalty m·|i − j| works at any distance, which is why it extrapolates to longer inputs (Press et al., 2022). RoPE rotates q and k so that their dot product sees only the offset.
