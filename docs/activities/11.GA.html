Attribution methods give each input word a share of the credit for a prediction. They often disagree, because each one answers a different question. You will compute four of them by hand on two tiny sentiment models and see where each one breaks.

Q1 What does each method actually measure?

Why these questions: they explain why attribution methods disagree on the same prediction, and how to check them.

  • Q1 → Stages 1–3: compute saliency, occlusion, integrated gradients and Shapley values by hand, then match each method to its question.
  • Q2 → Stages 1–2: a saturated model, and a model in which one word flips another.
  • Q3 → Stage 4: a sanity check on explanations (Adebayo et al., 2018), and a case where an explanation exposed a flawed model (Ribeiro et al., 2016).

Stage 4 — Can You Trust an Explanation?

Two published results: a sanity check that tests whether an explanation depends on the model at all, and a case where an explanation exposed a model that was right for the wrong reason.

Takeaway
  • Saliency is the slope at the input. When the output is saturated, it gives 0 even to words the prediction depends on.
  • Occlusion deletes one word at a time. When two words carry redundant evidence, each one looks unimportant on its own (0.2 here).
  • Shapley values average a word's contribution over every order of adding the words and always add up to f(x) − f(blank). Interactions get split: φbad = 0, although "bad" changes the score by ±0.4. The exact values need 2n model evaluations, so KernelSHAP samples them.
  • Integrated gradients collect the slope along a path from a blank input, so they also add up to the total (0.5 + 0.5 = 1).
  • Trust needs tests: an explanation that does not change when the weights are randomized ignores the model (Adebayo et al., 2018). The best use of explanations is catching models that are right for the wrong reason, like snow meaning "wolf".
